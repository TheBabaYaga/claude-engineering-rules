# No AI slop — the named patterns

Source: the `no-ai-slop` skill by Peter Yang, <https://github.com/petergyang/no-ai-slop>. MIT license. This file adapts the pattern list. The examples are written for this repository.

Each pattern has a name, a shape you can point at, and a fix. Name the pattern when you flag it. A name is evidence. A score or a guess about the author is not.

## 1. Binary contrast

The shape: "It is not X. It is Y." Also "The question is not X, it is Y" and "It is not just X but Y".

State Y. The contrast adds drama, not information.

> **Before:** The problem is not the model. It is the eval.
> **After:** The eval matters more than the model.

## 2. Throat-clearing opener

The shape: "Here is the thing", "Here is what I mean", "Let me be clear", "I will be honest", "The uncomfortable truth is".

Delete the opener. Start with the point.

> **Before:** Here is the thing: the cache never invalidates.
> **After:** The cache never invalidates.

## 3. Faux-insight setup

The shape: "What most people get wrong", "here is what nobody tells you", "the part everyone misses", "this is the part most people skip".

The setup makes the writer the only expert in the room. Delete it. The claim stands on its own.

> **Before:** The part everyone misses: distribution is the real moat.
> **After:** Distribution is the moat.

## 4. Colon reveal

The shape: a noun phrase, a colon, then a lowercase reveal. "The detail that makes it work: a second agent grades it."

Write a plain sentence. Use a colon for a list, a label, or a quote.

> **Before:** The best part: it learns from each run.
> **After:** It learns from each run.

Use sentence case after a colon. Capitalize only for grammar, a proper noun, a title, or code.

## 5. Superficial analysis

The shape: a trailing "-ing" clause that pretends to explain what a fact means. "highlighting", "underscoring", "reflecting", "showcasing", "demonstrating".

Give the consequence instead.

> **Before:** The release adds file search, highlighting the team's commitment to better workflows.
> **After:** The release adds file search, so a user can find an old draft without leaving the editor.

## 6. Importance puffery

The shape: "stands as a testament", "marks a pivotal moment", "plays a vital role", "solidifies its position", "underscores its significance".

Give the fact. The reader decides if it matters.

> **Before:** The launch marks a pivotal moment for the company.
> **After:** The launch is the company's first paid product.

## 7. Interpretive metadiscourse

The shape: a line that steps outside the subject to tell the reader what to notice. "That last part matters more than it sounds", "the key point is", "as you can see", "this distinction matters", and a redundant "in other words".

If the point is clear, delete the line. If it is not clear, add the fact that makes it clear.

## 8. Weasel attribution

The shape: "experts agree", "industry reports suggest", "many argue", "widely regarded as", "studies show".

Name the source, or cut the claim. Never invent a source. Ask instead.

## 9. Fake-strong verb

The shape: a weak noun phrase dressed as a strong verb. "serves as a centralized hub for", "acts as a bridge between".

"Is" and "has" are often the clearer verbs.

> **Before:** The app serves as a centralized hub for sponsor management.
> **After:** The app tracks sponsors, drafts, due dates, and approvals in one place.

## 10. Synonym cycling

The shape: one thing gets three names, for style.

Repeat the correct word. ASD-STE100 rule 1.11 says the same thing.

> **Before:** The agent reviews the draft. The assistant scores the piece. The tool suggests fixes.
> **After:** The agent reviews the draft, scores it, and suggests fixes.

## 11. Negative listing

The shape: "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z."

Say Z.

## 12. Dramatic fragmentation

The shape: "X. And Y. And Z." Also "That is it. That is the whole thing."

Write complete sentences.

## 13. Robotic rhythm

The shape: the same sentence shape three times, identical paragraph lengths, or a stack of punchy fragments.

Vary the shape when it helps the point. Do not vary it for decoration.

## 14. Rhetorical setup

The shape: "What if I told you", "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", and a question that the writer answers in the next line.

Delete the setup. Make the point.

## 15. Fake-profound kicker

The shape: a final line that turns the point into a metaphor, an aphorism, or a mic-drop sentence.

Delete it. Do not rewrite it into a better metaphor. Do not keep the rhythm. End on the clearest concrete sentence that the draft already has. If the end needs closure, add a plain takeaway or the next action.

## 16. Summary-recap ending

The shape: "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "Overall", or a last paragraph that repeats the piece.

The reader was just there. End on the last concrete point, a takeaway, or the next action.

## 17. Formatting slop

The shape: an emoji in a heading, bold in the middle of a sentence, a bullet list where two sentences of prose read better, a heading over a two-sentence section.

Format follows the content. It does not decorate it.

## 18. Em dash as a rhythm crutch

Do not use an em dash inside a sentence by default. Use a comma, a period, or parentheses.

- Short copy: none.
- A long document: one or two are acceptable when they clearly beat the alternatives.
- Never a cluster of them in one paragraph.

A label separator in a list or a table is not this pattern. This rule is about prose.
