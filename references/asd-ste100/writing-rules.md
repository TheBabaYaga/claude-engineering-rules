# ASD-STE100 — the 53 writing rules

Source: ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English, Issue 9 (15 January 2025). Part 1 gives 53 writing rules in 9 sections. Part 2 gives a dictionary of 875 approved words and 1274 words that are not approved, each with an approved alternative.

This file paraphrases the rules. It does not copy ASD's text, its explanations, or its examples. ## Section 1 — Words (14 rules)

**Which words can you use?**

- **1.1 [D]** — Use only three types of word: a word that the dictionary approves, a technical noun, or a technical verb.
- **1.2 [D]** — Use an approved word only as the part of speech that the dictionary gives it.
- **1.3 [D]** — Use an approved word only with the meaning that the dictionary gives it.
- **1.4 [D]** — Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives.

**Technical nouns**

- **1.5 [D]** — You can use a word if it fits one of the technical noun categories of the standard. Issue 9 has 22 categories. It added "Law and regulations" and "Animals, plants, and other life forms".
- **1.6 [D]** — Use a word that the dictionary does not approve only when it is a technical noun, or a part of one.
- **1.7** — Do not use a technical noun as a verb.
- **1.8** — Use the technical nouns that your company, your industry, or your subject field approves.
- **1.9** — When you can select, take the technical noun that is short and easy to understand.
- **1.10** — Do not use a regional word, slang, or jargon as a technical noun.
- **1.11** — Give one item one name. Do not use two different technical nouns for the same item.

**Technical verbs**

- **1.12 [D]** — You can use a verb if it fits one of the technical verb categories of the standard.
- **1.13** — Do not use a technical verb as a noun.

**Spelling**

- **1.14** — Use American English spelling, unless an official directive tells you differently.

## Section 2 — Multi-word nouns (2 rules)

- **2.1** — Write a multi-word noun of three words or less.
- **2.2** — When a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full first. Then make it clear with a short form, or with hyphens between the words that operate as one unit.

## Section 3 — Verbs (7 rules)

**Verb forms and tenses**

- **3.1 [D]** — Use only the verb forms that the dictionary gives.
- **3.2** — Use only these forms: the infinitive, the imperative (command) form, the simple present tense, the simple past tense, the simple future tense, and the past participle as an adjective.
- **3.3** — Use the past participle form as an adjective.
- **3.4** — Do not use auxiliary verbs to make a complex verb construction. This removes the present perfect, the past perfect, and the continuous forms.
- **3.5** — Use an "-ing" form only as a technical noun, or as a modifier in a technical noun.

**Active voice**

- **3.6** — Use the active voice. In descriptive text, you can use the passive voice only when the agent is unknown.

**How to describe an action**

- **3.7 [D]** — Use an approved verb for an action. Do not use a noun, or a different part of speech, for that action.

## Section 4 — Sentences (5 rules)

- **4.1** — Write short and clear sentences.
- **4.2** — Do not remove words, and do not use contractions, to make a sentence shorter.
- **4.3** — Use a vertical list for complex text.
- **4.4** — Use connecting words and connecting phrases to join sentences that contain related topics.
- **4.5** — Where it applies, put an article ("the", "a", "an") or a demonstrative adjective ("this", "these") before a noun or a multi-word noun.

## Section 5 — Procedural writing (5 rules)

- **5.1** — Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence. A warning and a caution obey this rule too.
- **5.2** — Write one instruction in each sentence, unless two or more actions occur at the same time.
- **5.3** — Write an instruction in the imperative (command) form.
- **5.4** — When the reader must know a condition first, start the instruction with the descriptive statement. Then divide that statement from the command with a comma.
- **5.5** — Write a note to give information only. A note does not give an instruction.

## Section 6 — Descriptive writing (6 rules)

**Content structure**

- **6.1** — Give the information gradually. Keep one subject in each sentence.
- **6.2** — Use key words and key phrases to give the text a logical structure.

**Sentences**

- **6.3** — Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence.

**Paragraphs**

- **6.4** — Use a paragraph to show related information.
- **6.5** — Give each paragraph one topic.
- **6.6** — Keep each paragraph to six sentences or less.

## Section 7 — Safety instructions (3 rules)

- **7.1** — Use the applicable word to identify the level of risk. A warning tells the reader that there is a risk of injury or death. A caution tells the reader that there is a risk of damage to objects.
- **7.2** — Start a safety instruction with a clear and accurate command or condition.
- **7.3** — Give an explanation that shows the risk, or the possible result.

## Section 8 — Punctuation and word count (7 rules)

**Punctuation**

- **8.1** — You can use all standard English punctuation marks, but not the semicolon (;). The standard bans that one mark only. The em dash stays permitted.
- **8.2** — Use a hyphen (-) to connect words that relate directly.
- **8.3** — You can use parentheses for these: a reference to an illustration or a text, a letter or number that identifies an item, the identifier of a work step, an abbreviation, the singular and the plural form together, an explanation of a word or a part of a sentence, and an alternative.

**Word count**

- **8.4** — In a vertical list, a colon (:) ends a sentence for the word count, the same as a period.
- **8.5** — Text in parentheses counts as one word in its sentence.
- **8.6** — Count each of these as one word: a number, a number with its unit of measurement, an abbreviation, an alphanumeric identifier, quoted text, a title or heading or placard or label, and a proper noun of a person, a group, an organization, or a geopolitical entity.
- **8.7** — A hyphenated word counts as one word.

## Section 9 — Writing practices (4 rules)

- **9.1** — When a word-for-word replacement is not sufficient, write the sentence with a different construction.
- **9.2 [D]** — Use each approved word correctly.
- **9.3** — When you put two words together, do not make a phrasal verb.
- **9.4** — Keep a consistent style when you select terminology and wording.

## Section 9 — General recommendations (8 items)

These are recommendations, not numbered rules.

- **GR-1 — The conjunction "that".** Keep "that" before a subordinate clause. It shows the reader where the main clause stops. Many languages cannot omit the equivalent word, so it also helps translation. Write "Make sure that the valve is open", not "Make sure the valve is open".
- **GR-2 — The preposition "with".** STE approves three meanings: association, help or sharing, and a means or instrument. Read each sentence again for ambiguity. "Install the panel with the green fasteners" has three possible meanings.
- **GR-3 — Pronouns.** Use only the pronouns in the dictionary. "He" and "she" are not permitted. When a pronoun can refer to more than one noun, replace it with the noun.
- **GR-4 — The pronoun "this".** Make sure that the reader knows what "this" refers to. If it can refer to more than one item, give the context again.
- **GR-5 — False friends.** A false friend looks like a word in your native language but has a different meaning in English. Check the English meaning.
- **GR-6 — Latin abbreviations.** Do not use "e.g.", "i.e.", or "etc.". Write "for example", "that is", and "and so on". Or remove them.
- **GR-7 — Inclusive language.** Use gender-neutral language. STE does not permit "he", "she", "man", or "woman", unless the context makes the word necessary.
- **GR-8 — Possessive form.** STE permits the Saxon genitive ("the manufacturer's instructions"), but many languages have no equivalent. If you are not sure that the sentence is correct, use "of" instead.
